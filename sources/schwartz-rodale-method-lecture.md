A BEGINNER'S COURSE · BUILT FROM THE FULL RECORDING — THE SCHWARTZ METHOD

In the 1990s, the highest-paid advertising writer in America stood up in front of a publisher's staff and explained, step by step, exactly how he did his job. This is that lecture, taught from the beginning — in plain language, with his own words as the proof.

Speaker Eugene Schwartz (1927–1995) · Venue Rodale Press staff workshop · Runtime 1:26:31 · Lessons 12

## Start here

You do not need to know anything about advertising to follow this. Here is the whole situation in five sentences.

Eugene Schwartz sold things through the post. A publisher would give him a book. He would write an envelope, a letter and some leaflets, all stuffed in one packet, and millions of those packets would be posted to strangers. If enough strangers posted money back, the book was a success and he got paid very well.

This is the hardest possible test of writing, because there is no salesperson, no shop, and no second chance. The words either work or they don't, and you find out precisely, in money. Schwartz did it for forty years and became the highest-paid writer in the business.

What makes this recording unusual is that he gives away the boring part. Most successful people describe their results. Schwartz describes his actual working day — the timer on his desk, the pencil in his hand, the two weeks of tedium before he writes a single word. He tells the room he is going to be "completely transparent," and then he is.

Almost everything he teaches transfers directly to writing an email, a landing page, an ad, or a video script today. The delivery method changed. The reasoning did not.

## Twelve words he uses that you may not know

**Mail order** — Selling something by posting an advertisement to a person's home and having them post money back. The whole industry this lecture comes from.
**Package** — Everything in one envelope: the envelope itself, the letter, the leaflets, the order form. Schwartz wrote the whole package, not just a headline.
**Copy** — The words in an advertisement. A "copywriter" writes them.
**Copy cub** — An old term for a beginner copywriter. Schwartz uses it kindly.
**Claim** — A specific promise the product makes. "Lowers your blood pressure in nine days" is a claim. Claims are the raw material of everything he does.
**Mechanism** — The how behind the claim — the strange method that makes it work. This turns out to be the most important idea in the lecture.
**To pull** — To get responses. "It pulled well" means lots of people ordered. "It stopped pulling" means the advert died.
**The list** — The set of names and addresses being mailed. Companies rented lists from each other.
**Premium** — A free bonus gift included to persuade someone to order — a small booklet, usually.
**Order blank** — The form you fill in and post back. The finish line of the entire package.
**Fatigue** — What happens when an advert has been mailed so many times it stops working.
**Rodale** — The publisher he is speaking to — health and natural-remedy books, and Prevention magazine. Their customers were mostly older and interested in natural health.

## His record at this publisher, from the introduction

27 winners out of 30 packages. Someone in the room says 28 later; he corrects them down to 27.
Around 8 million books sold by his adverts alone.
About $200 million in revenue generated for that one publisher.
He worked three hours a day. This is not a boast about grinding. It is the punchline of Lesson 2.

## LESSON 01 · 06:21 – 18:39 — Talent is not the edge. Preparation is.

IN PLAIN ENGLISH

Schwartz starts by taking away the flattering explanation. He is not, he says, unusually talented or unusually creative. Other writers at that publisher were better than him. He won because he prepared more thoroughly than anyone he was tested against.

And he really was tested. He explains that Rodale would hire two writers on the same book, not tell either who the other one was, and mail both packages to see which won. He calls it the Super Bowl. You cannot out-imagine a room like that. You can out-work it.

IN HIS OWN WORDS

> "I got that not because I'm extremely talented or extremely creative or anything like that. I like to think of myself as working harder than anybody I'm tested against." — 07:07

> "The person who is the best prepared and the most knowledgeable makes the most money… The will to win means nothing without the will to prepare." — 17:53, quoting Red Blaik, the West Point football coach

HIS ACTUAL PROCESS, STEP BY STEP

This is the part almost nobody copies, and it is the most valuable thing in the lecture. He is handed a manuscript of about 1,100 pages and given five weeks. Here is what he does with them.

1. **Throw away the table of contents.** He puts it aside without reading it. He does not want somebody else's map of the book, and he deliberately arrives with no opinion of his own. He wants the good bits to catch him by surprise.
2. **Read every page and mark every claim in red pencil.** A mark at the start and end of each promise the book makes. Some pages give three; some give none. He does this for a maximum of one hour a day, because after an hour you stop actually reading. That is why it takes two weeks.
3. **Have all the marked bits typed out.** They come back as 50–60 pages of nothing but promises. He calls this the vocabulary. It is the raw material for everything that follows.
4. **Catch ideas on scraps of paper as you go.** Ideas arrive while reading. He scribbles each one down in his own shorthand and puts it aside. He does not act on any of them yet.
5. **Let the file size force you to organise.** Fifty pages in one document makes the computer slow and unhappy, so he breaks it into blocks — and that annoyance ends up doing his first round of sorting for him.
6. **Write the whole body with no headline at all.** No headline, no subheadings, nothing in big type. Just claims, arranged. Three weeks have now gone and the client is starting to ring.
7. **Only now, pick the strongest claims and build the envelope.** The headline is chosen from finished material, not invented on a blank page. This is week four.
8. **Go back through it against the book.** Check everything again. Mark where something is missing or where a line hasn't landed yet. Deliver in week five.

THE STANDARD HE HOLDS HIMSELF TO

He tells a story to show you what "prepared" means. He sent copy to a doctor he'd never worked with. The doctor crossed out the word eggs, saying his book never claimed eggs could be controlled. Schwartz rang him and quoted the sentence on page 164. The doctor laughed — and Schwartz says that laugh made him confident the advert would win, because it meant he knew the book better than the man who wrote it.

Then he draws the conclusion that justifies the whole two weeks:

> "The power of the ad… is always in the product itself. It's not in the copywriter. The copywriter simply finds it and expresses it." — 15:30

He goes further: give him a brilliant but careless writer against a careful beginner, and he'll back the beginner two times out of three.

WHAT THIS MEANS FOR YOU

Before you write anything, spend real time collecting every specific promise the thing you're selling can make — from the product, the manual, the reviews, the customer emails, wherever they live. Write them down in one place. Do it in short sessions, because attention fades.

The test of whether you've done enough: could you correct the person who made the product about their own product? If not, you are not ready to write yet.

## LESSON 02 · 18:39 – 24:51 — The timer, and why he never had writer's block

IN PLAIN ENGLISH

Schwartz claims he has never once had writer's block — across nine books, thirty packages for this publisher, and dozens more elsewhere. He says he has never stared at a blank page for more than a few minutes.

His solution is a cheap kitchen timer. But the timer is not really the trick. The trick is what he removes: the requirement to produce anything.

WHAT HE ACTUALLY DOES EACH MORNING

Same desk, for twenty-eight years. Never at the office — always at home. He aims for 9:30 and usually starts at 10:00. He takes out three pens. He brings one cup of coffee, and he mentions, seriously, that the little jug of cream has to be there so he can pour it and stir it. He opens the file. Then he sets the timer to 33.33 minutes and presses start.

> "This is the Liberator. This is the thing that frees you from all the anxiety and all the lack of self-confidence in the world. It is a timer… I have no goal for the next 33.33 minutes except to work on the copy." — 22:33

During those minutes he has one permission and one rule:

- He does not have to work. He says so twice. He can sit. He can stare. He can drink the coffee and stare some more.
- He may not get up, and may not do any other task. Not even write his own name.

And then the honest bit. What breaks the deadlock is not willpower — it's boredom, and it takes about one or two minutes. Bored, he starts scrolling through the file. Something reaches out and grabs him: this claim is good, but it's not quite phrased right — wouldn't it be better if we cut these words? And he's working. The test, the money, the rent are all gone, because none of them was ever the goal.

> "You should be so absorbed that you shouldn't notice the time passing." — 24:06, on why the timer must ring out loud

Six of those blocks makes three hours. That is his entire working day. He stops because after three hours he's tired — and, crucially, three good hours are enough because he did the preparation in Lesson 1.

WHAT THIS MEANS FOR YOU

Blank-page paralysis usually isn't laziness — it's pressure. You sit down carrying the deadline, the client, the fear that it won't be good. Schwartz's fix is to make the session's only requirement be present with the material.

Try one session tomorrow where the goal is not "draft the hook" but simply "be in the file for 33 minutes, allowed to produce nothing." The permission is the active ingredient. A block with a deliverable attached is just a shorter deadline, and it will feel exactly the same as before.

## LESSON 03 · 24:51 – 44:05 — Working and creating are two different states

IN PLAIN ENGLISH

Between each 33-minute block, Schwartz takes five minutes off. That break is not a rest — it is the other half of the method, and he thinks it's where the ideas actually come from. He stops his own lecture to demonstrate it, telling the room they will halt at the thirty-minute mark and deliberately do nothing useful.

> "When you're working you're not creating. The whole secret is to know when you're working, and leave work, and go on to creation." — 25:36

HE THROWS OUT THE WORD "CREATIVITY"

He says he despises the word because there's no picture inside it. God created heaven and earth out of nothing — you can't make something from nothing, so the word is useless as an instruction. He wants what he calls operational words: words you can actually work from. So he swaps it for a better one.

> "Creativity to me is what I call connectivity… the ability to take two ideas that already exist, that are in two different sentences or even in two different fields, and put them together, connect them." — 31:46

This is a genuinely useful reframe for a beginner. You are not being asked to summon something from nowhere. You are being asked to pair two things that already exist — and a pairing nobody has made before is what stops a reader.

FOCUSED MIND, UNFOCUSED MIND

He also junks "conscious" and "unconscious" as vague, and replaces them with focused and unfocused. His model: your focused attention holds only about seven things at once — which is why we invented writing that runs along a line, breaks, and runs along another. Around those seven is a much wider pool of material drifting about unattended.

Logic happens inside the seven. New connections happen out in the pool. So the only way to reach the pool is to give the focused part of your mind something to do that doesn't need it.

His illustration is Mozart, said to have thrown a billiard ball off three cushions and written one note while it travelled — the unpredictable path trapping his attention and freeing the rest of his mind. (This story is almost certainly a myth; see "What to question" at the end. The technique still works.)

His own version is far more practical, and much easier to copy:

- He has written more headlines while shaving than at his desk.
- He keeps notepads all over the house, and carries a pen into the shower.
- Most of his best adverts and books were written on holiday, in the margins of other people's books — sitting in front of the sea, working on nothing, while ideas arrive faster than he can write them down.

NEVER WORK ON ONE THING AT A TIME

He always runs three projects at once — one book, one article, one advert, an hour each. That is where the three hours come from. Working on a biography of Jesus, he says, delivers ideas for a health book out of nowhere. He credits the psychologist Gardner and calls it a creative triangle: while your focused mind is on one project, the unfocused part quietly works the others. A three-sided game of ping-pong.

WHAT THIS MEANS FOR YOU

Two things you can do this week. First: stop treating the walk, the shower and the commute as time away from work — they are where the connections get made. Carry something to write on, always.

Second: schedule two unrelated live projects back to back instead of one. It sounds like a distraction and Schwartz insists it's the opposite. It's easy to test — just notice whether ideas for the first arrive while you're inside the second.

## LESSON 04 · 25:36 – 28:41 — You do not write copy. You assemble it.

IN PLAIN ENGLISH

This is the line he says with the most force in the whole lecture, and it's aimed squarely at beginners who think good advertising comes from clever phrasing.

> "Copy is not written. If anyone tells you they write copy, sneer at them. Copy is not written — copy is assembled. You do not write copy, you assemble it. You are working with a series of building blocks." — 25:36

The blocks are the claims you collected in Lesson 1, plus images and desires. His picture of the finished package is worth keeping: you are building a small city of desire for the reader to come and live in. Not a document — a place.

WORDS ARE WINDOWS

The thing that tells him an advert is in trouble is, surprisingly, a compliment. Somebody tells him a headline is beautiful and asks where he found those gorgeous words. His reaction is despair — because that person looked at the words instead of through them.

> "Words in advertising are like the windows in a store. You must be able to look right through them and see the product. If you see the window, it's dirty… and you're going to lose." — 27:09

Copy should never draw attention to itself. The reader should never notice they're reading sentences at all — what should reach them is the picture the words carry. If you want the words themselves admired, he says, leave advertising and write novels.

He then proves it on his own most famous line. "Sneaky little arthritis tricks." Four words. Not one of them unusual. All completely ordinary. The power is entirely in the combination — which is exactly what he meant by connectivity in Lesson 3.

WHAT THIS MEANS FOR YOU

A practical way to use this: when you finish a draft, ask of each line, am I proud of how this sounds, or of what it makes the reader see? Lines you're proud of for their sound are the ones to check hardest.

And if you feel stuck for words, that's usually the wrong diagnosis. You're short of blocks — go back and collect more claims.

## LESSON 05 · 28:41 – 36:25 — Worked example: how four famous words happened

IN PLAIN ENGLISH

Here he does the thing that makes this recording valuable: he rebuilds the thinking behind his best-known headline, and then shows what each part of the envelope was for. If you only study one section, make it this one.

THE SITUATION

Rodale had built its business on books by doctors, or by people following doctors. Then a manuscript arrived built from around 700 people who had gone away from doctors — readers who had written in to say the medical route failed them and they'd fixed themselves.

Reading those letters, Schwartz found the emotional fact nobody had put into words. These people got wonderful results — and felt guilty about it. They still relied on their doctors. They worried about being found out; about the doctor discovering it and taking away their strong medication. That guilt is the thing he wrote into.

Notice that sneaky is a word he says should never appear in advertising — sneaking around is unappealing and nobody wants to be described that way. It works here only because it names, exactly, the position the reader was already in.

And he insists he did not assemble the line. Deep in the material, off in left field, the four words simply arrived by themselves.

> "The Muse means that your unconscious mind is delivering something to you… What do you do with it? If you want to set new records in sales with it, you have to first put it up, and then you see what can I say after that." — 34:54

THE ENVELOPE, TAKEN APART

RODALE ARTHRITIS PACKAGE — FRONT OF ENVELOPE

**"Sneaky little arthritis tricks"** — Line 1, the hook. A wild, intriguing promise made of four ordinary words. Its only job is to get you to read line 2. It doesn't explain, prove, or sell anything.

**"…with natural foods and do-it-yourself secrets that have pain-proofed over 100 men and women like you"** — Line 2, makes it believable and doable. Watch how much work one sentence does. It names the method (natural foods, do-it-yourself), states the result (pain-proofed), and — most importantly — moves the risk off the reader: 100 people already proved it, so you don't have to be the one taking the chance.

Photo captions: "All symptoms disappeared and did not return." · "After another 10 days I felt cured, and even the rheumatoid nodules were disappearing." · "I have found my salvation — it was my idea, not the doctor's, and it's better than any medicine he has prescribed."

The photo captions — stand-ins for the reader. Real faces: a woman walking, a man being kissed by a child. Schwartz's term for these is case histories, and he defines one precisely: proof from somebody who acted as your surrogate, taking the risk so the reader doesn't have to. Note the third caption — it answers the guilt head-on and turns going against the doctor into something to be proud of.

Back of envelope: "three youth pills, given away — see other age-reversal miracles inside" — The back buys the opening. It gives an explicit reason to open the envelope. He states the problem plainly: the big job is simply getting them to open it.

His summary of the structure is two lines long, and you can apply it today: the first line makes the wild claim, the second line makes it doable.

One more thing worth noticing. Inside, the six-page letter is nothing but case histories, one after another. And the separate leaflet is entirely what doctors say about the method — so the same package that invites you to defy your doctor also hands you doctors' approval. That is not sloppiness. That is answering the guilt from both directions.

WHAT THIS MEANS FOR YOU

Before writing a hook, go and find the thing your customer feels but hasn't said out loud — the awkward, slightly embarrassing part of their situation. Schwartz didn't find "people want less pain." He found "people are secretly disobeying their doctor and feel guilty about it." The first is a category. The second is a headline.

Then build in two lines, not one: claim, then method plus proof plus permission.

## LESSON 06 · 44:52 – 52:32 — Every line has one job: earn the next line

IN PLAIN ENGLISH

He opens by acting out something everyone recognises — standing over his own post, dealing it into two piles. One pile is dead: you have lost, that person will never open it. The other is alive. Everything in the package exists to walk the reader from the envelope to the order form.

Then he attacks the rule he was taught as a young man. Back then, the accepted wisdom was: one headline, it should lead into the copy, and it should sell the product or make one overwhelming promise. The key word was power. He says, flatly, I hate that.

> "The headline has absolutely nothing in the world to do except one thing: get you to read the sentence underneath the headline. The sentence underneath the headline has absolutely nothing in the world to do except one thing: get you to read the sentence that follows it." — 46:23

So the headline should not try to sell, should not identify the company, should not deliver an overwhelming claim. Everything else is secondary. What you are building is a habit of reading — one line, then the next, then the next.

This is enormously freeing for a beginner. You are not trying to write the perfect headline. You are trying to write a line that makes the next line unavoidable.

THE ENVELOPE HAS REPLACED THE HEADLINE

His conclusion for direct mail: the front and back of the envelope together are the sales pitch. You can put hundreds of words there. They don't need to be headlines. They only need to be tantalising. He holds up an envelope with about 30 claims on the front and nearly 50 on the back, apologises to the art department for what he made them lay out, and calls it a wonderful selling medium.

ALWAYS LEAVE AN EXIT

Two jobs run at once inside the package. For the reader who wants everything, pile belief on belief and desire on desire. But for the reader who is about to stop, give an escape route straight to the order form at any moment — which is what the money-back guarantee and the 21-day trial are there for.

That is also his answer to the question every beginner asks:

> "The copy should be as long as you need to contain the claims and not bankrupt the company that sends you out." — 51:47

With one condition attached: because the reader can leave at any point, the envelope alone, and the first page alone, each have to be a complete enough case to make somebody try.

WHAT THIS MEANS FOR YOU

Read your draft one sentence at a time and ask only: does this make the next sentence feel necessary? That single test will fix more copy than any amount of rewriting for elegance.

"How long should it be" is the wrong question. The right ones are: have I run out of real claims, and can someone who quits halfway still buy?

## LESSON 07 · 52:32 – 53:19 — Never sell. Ask them to try.

IN PLAIN ENGLISH

Short lesson, big consequences. He says it with five "evers" in a row, which tells you how much he means it.

> "You're never selling anything. You never, ever, ever, ever, ever sell anything in mail order. You always ask the person to try something." — 52:32

His reasoning: the book will sell itself once it's in their hands. Yes, returns go up when you make it that easy — and he says that's wonderful, because at least they tried it. The entire purpose of the package is to produce a trial, by whatever means available.

This is why the guarantee and the free trial period aren't small print in his packages. They're the mechanism. He isn't asking someone to decide they want the book. He's asking them to agree to look at it.

WHAT THIS MEANS FOR YOU

Look at what you're actually asking someone to do, and see whether you can make it smaller. "Buy this" is a large decision made by a stranger who doesn't trust you yet. "Try this, and send it back if you don't like it" is a much smaller one — and it moves the risk from them to you, which is the same move as the case histories in Lesson 5.

Accepting some returns is the price of that, and he considers it obviously worth paying.

## LESSON 08 · 53:19 – 56:22 — Write to one person, and use the word "you"

IN PLAIN ENGLISH

He calls "who are you writing to?" an absolutely essential question, and gives a two-part answer where both halves matter.

> "You're writing to an individual, a single person, always — who shares a problem or a desire with a huge mass of other people." — 53:19

The single person keeps the writing intimate — you're speaking to one human being, not addressing a crowd. The shared problem is what makes it a business — arthritis, held by millions, is why the mailing is worth sending at all. Beginners usually get one half and lose the other: either warm writing aimed at nobody in particular, or cold writing aimed at a demographic.

That leads him to the word he considers the most powerful in the language — and it isn't the one people expect.

> "The most powerful word in the English language is not yes, and it's not free — it's you." — 54:04

He gives a rule so mechanical you can run it over a finished draft. He says he goes crazy reading instructions like take the hands and apply the cream to the face — because there are no the hands and no the face. There are only your hands and your face. The first pass a copywriter makes over material written by somebody else is to change every the into your.

USE THEIR WORDS, EVEN WHEN YOU DON'T UNDERSTAND THEM

Writing the arthritis package, Schwartz had never known anyone with arthritis properly. The letters were full of drug names and clinical terms that meant nothing to him. His decision goes against what most people are taught about writing simply:

> "I determined that I would try in the copy to use as many technical terms as possible, because those people were familiar with those technical terms. They had been with doctors for years, and failed." — 55:36

The point is not that jargon is good. The point is that those particular words were their words, and using them proved he came from inside their experience. "Write simply" means write in the reader's language — which is sometimes more technical than yours, not less.

WHAT THIS MEANS FOR YOU

Two passes you can run on any draft today. One: replace every the with your where it refers to the reader's own life. Two: find five words your customers genuinely use — from reviews, support tickets, forum posts — and check whether any of them appear in your copy. Usually they don't, because you replaced them with your industry's words.

## LESSON 09 · 56:22 – 1:05:42 — Listening, and the danger of your own success

IN PLAIN ENGLISH

This is the most personal stretch of the lecture, and it contains a warning most successful people never say out loud.

> "The greatest asset after hard work is the ability to listen." — 57:09

You have to know the person you're asking something from — well enough, he says, that you can sound like them and they mistake you for their own reflection. He lists three layers to listen to: the person with the problem, the part of society they come from, and the society itself. At a party, in a taxi, on the train — ask questions, show appreciation, listen.

THE WARNING

Then he turns it on himself. He spent his first fifteen years in Butte, Montana, and says he still thinks and feels the way most of the country does. Then he moved to New York, became extremely sophisticated, and now keeps sophisticated company — which he calls horrible, professionally. He constantly works to get away from it.

The message to the room: as you become more successful, you drift away from the people you're selling to, and you have to deliberately rebuild contact with that world. It won't happen by itself.

HIS HOMEWORK LIST

- The National Enquirer, every word. His line: there are your headlines, there are your people and their feelings.
- Vanity Fair, and the tabloid television shows.
- Any film taking $100M+, watched two or three times.
- The hit songs of the 50s and 60s — all of them, for an older audience — plus the current teenage top ten.

WRITE LIKE AN ACTION FILM

His most transferable structural idea, and it comes from cinema. Take a stopwatch to Lethal Weapon: roughly every three minutes there's another blast — a crash, an explosion — then a few minutes of dialogue that means nothing, then another one. He tells the room to write to that rhythm.

> "This letter is not a series of paragraphs. This letter is a series of headlines… every one of them can be taken and put up here. You've got to give them the punch." — 1:01:01

The justification is fair-minded: you are asking someone to spend fifteen or twenty minutes with you in a busy day. You owe them a charge, and another charge, and another.

HIDDEN DESIRES

Asked how he'd sell a $99 investment newsletter to respectable people, his answer was one word: Gekko — the ruthless financier from the film Wall Street. Every reader of that newsletter, he says, wants to be Gekko: dishonest and powerful, but somehow still square, honest, and never caught. His point isn't cynicism. It's completeness — people are driven by things they won't admit to, sometimes shameful things, and pretending otherwise makes weak copy.

BE DRAMATIC — BECAUSE ATTENTION IS PARTIAL, NOT BECAUSE PEOPLE ARE STUPID

He pairs the instruction to be melodramatic with a genuinely warm defence of his audience, and the pairing is the point. His customers, he says, are the finest people in the world: fair, honest, wanting good things for their children, and much more intelligent than people assume. His proof is small and convincing — when his company accidentally posts two copies of a book, people pay $2.50 out of their own pocket to send one back.

So the reason to be dramatic is not that readers are dim. It's that they're giving you part of their attention.

> "It's like a painting on a wall. If you make the painting very small… they can't see it. So you make it very big, and with a great deal of contrast… So you have to be melodramatic — and be fair, and be honest, and fill your stuff with proof." — 1:04:54

He also frames the offer itself at that scale: put the reader in the middle, with hell on one side and heaven on the other. For this book: the agony of arthritis, against a pain-free healthy life for the next fifty years.

WHAT THIS MEANS FOR YOU

The listening instruction is the one to act on first, because it's free. Spend real time where your customers talk — not in analytics, but in their actual words. Schwartz's whole arthritis breakthrough came from reading letters closely enough to notice guilt.

And check your draft for the three-minute rule: mark every point where something genuinely lands. If there are long stretches between marks, you have paragraphs where you needed headlines.

## LESSON 10 · 1:05:42 – 1:08:48 — The engine: claim against mechanism

IN PLAIN ENGLISH

If you take one idea from this whole lecture, take this one. He calls it the engine that moves mail-order copy.

> "The engine that moves mail order copy can be summarised as the contrast between the claim and the mechanism." — 1:05:42

Two parts. The claim is what you promise. The mechanism is how it's done. The power comes from the gap between them — a promise that sounds reasonable, delivered by a method that sounds impossible, so the reader cannot settle the question in their own head. The only way to resolve it is to get the product.

SPECIMEN — FROM AN ADVERT THAT RAN 8–10 YEARS

"A way to vacuum dust from drawers without removing their contents"

Why it works. The claim is small and domestic — nobody's life is transformed by a clean drawer. But the method sounds impossible, and there's an itch you can only scratch by turning to page 71. Notice how unimpressive the promise is. That's deliberate.

CLAIM EXHAUSTION — WHY THIS MATTERS MORE OVER TIME

His diagnosis of what happens to every market as it matures. There are only so many ways to say you'll cure arthritis, only so many pounds you can promise someone will lose. Eventually every claim sounds like every other claim — including your own older adverts — and the whole category blurs into noise.

When that happens, the claim can no longer do the work. The mechanism has to.

He names Boardroom as the master of this, and Mel Martin as its great practitioner. Look at one of their pieces and the promises are almost mild — what never to eat on an aeroplane, when it's fine to pay a bill late. Pleasant, not overwhelming. He says most copywriters can't work out why they perform so well. The answer is an implied mechanism the reader has to see. His favourite: you can't cash an unsigned cheque — right? Wrong. See page 72.

THE TECHNIQUE HE CALLS "CAN'T BE DONE"

Imply a mechanism for a claim — and he is specific here — a medium-sized claim, not a huge one, because medium-sized is more believable. Then present something that simply cannot be done. The reader has to send for the book to find out how.

> "He will even send for the book if he doesn't believe you, because he wants to prove you wrong — because it itches, and he can't sleep at night." — 1:08:01

This is a subtle and powerful point. Curiosity that survives disbelief is stronger than persuasion, because you no longer need the reader to trust you. His example headline in this style: unspoken rules that will make or break your career — which works because it contradicts itself. How can you follow a rule nobody has spoken? You have to find out.

He calls this escalating strangeness a twist, and says the more crowded a field becomes, the harder you have to twist.

WHAT THIS MEANS FOR YOU

Take your three best claims and, for each, write the mechanism as a question the reader cannot answer from the advert alone. Then check it against his size rule: if the claim is enormous and the mechanism is strange, you've built disbelief rather than curiosity. Big claim needs a plain mechanism. Strange mechanism needs a modest claim.

Most beginner copy fails here by having a huge claim and no mechanism at all — which gives the reader nothing to be curious about, only something to doubt.

## LESSON 11 · 1:09:35 – 1:12:40 — When an advert dies, sell a different set of jobs

IN PLAIN ENGLISH

Every product eventually stops responding. It sells enormously the first time, less the second, and after enough mailings it fails. He calls this the fatigue factor, treats it as inevitable, and gives two responses.

The first is procedural: hand the product to new writers. The second is a reframe that tells them what to look for.

> "A product is not a book. You do not sell books, you do not sell magazines, you do not sell newsletters — you sell bundles of do's. Functions. Things that the thing will do." — 1:11:06

So when a product gets tired, you don't rewrite the advertisement. You go back into the product and find an entirely different set of jobs it does — and effectively sell a new product.

THE EXAMPLE, AND HIS HONESTY ABOUT IT

He is generous about what he was up against. The existing package for Healing Foods — doctors identify the 75 most healing foods, big carrots on the cover — he calls one of the best pieces of copy and one of the best layouts he has ever seen in his life. He says the only way to compete was to go completely around it: throw out everything the previous writer had done. He went back in for a fresh set of jobs — food cures, which hadn't been emphasised before, with doctors' quotes down the side — and beat it.

He then adds, unprompted, that the old piece had gone a bit tired by then. It's a small moment of intellectual honesty worth noticing — he doesn't let himself take full credit.

> "Never say a product is dead unless you've exhausted all its bundles of do." — 1:11:52

WHAT THIS MEANS FOR YOU

When something stops performing, the instinct is to change the words. Schwartz says change what you are selling — while keeping the same product. List every distinct job it does for someone, and notice how many you've never built an advert around. That list is your next few campaigns.

## LESSON 12 · 1:12:40 – 1:26:31 — Three ideas from the questions at the end

HE DOES NO RESEARCH AT ALL

Asked about audience research, he calls himself "extremely primitive" next to a sophisticated corporation and says it plainly: no focus groups, no demographics, nothing. He writes an advert, tests it, and occasionally tests something new against it. That is the entire apparatus.

Worth understanding correctly, because this line gets misused. He isn't against measurement — he mailed competing packages to real lists and counted the orders, which is a rigorous test. He's against asking people in advance what they'd like. What replaces it is Lesson 1 and Lesson 9: two weeks inside the material, and a lifetime of deliberately listening to ordinary people.

COMMITMENT PREDICTS COMMITMENT

The best idea in the questions. Rodale's customers acquired through two-minute TV adverts didn't perform well on his mailing lists, and he explains why in terms of what the buyer spent — not money, time.

Someone who works through one of his packages gives thirty minutes to the mailing and more to the book. That person has committed something. A two-minute TV buyer — however clever the advert — has committed two minutes. His prediction: run a thirty-minute infomercial instead, and those buyers would perform well for you.

> "The reader, the prospect, the buyer must make a commitment towards your product. And if you have a similar commitment, you'll have a similar type of sale." — 1:21:53

This is a genuinely modern idea and it transfers straight across. How much effort someone spent to become your customer predicts how they'll behave afterwards.

FREE GIFTS, AND PLAYING POOL

He is completely sold on premiums — free bonus gifts — and says he wouldn't send anything without one, often two. His reasoning is unsentimental: some people will order the book purely to get the free gift, open the parcel, keep the gift and send the book back. He counts that as a win, because the whole object is to produce a trial for any reason at all.

He admires how Rodale manufactures them — recycling old book chapters into new booklets, which he calls irresistible. He also notes a real disagreement without resolving it: he picks gifts unrelated to the book's subject; Rodale picks closely related ones. Both work, and he says he doesn't know which is better. (Notice that he's willing to say that.)

> "You never sell in mail order anything once. You always are going to the next sale. You're always playing pool — you're always sinking the ball, and at the same time you're getting your position on the next shot." — 1:24:10

The warning attached: premium overload. When every line on the envelope says yours free, the pile starts working against you. Watch results closely, because in this business response often doesn't drift downward gently — it plummets.

## The whole course on one page

Every rule he states, and what it's actually for. If you print one thing, print this.

- **Preparation beats talent.** He won 27 of 30 against better writers by out-preparing them, not out-imagining them.
- **Collect every claim before writing a word.** 1,100 pages, red pencil, one hour a day, two weeks — down to 50 pages of pure promise.
- **Throw away the table of contents.** Arrive with no map and no opinion, so the good material surprises you.
- **Know the product better than its maker.** The test of readiness. The power is in the product; you only find it.
- **Write the body first. Headlines come last.** A headline is chosen from finished claims, never invented on a blank page.
- **33 minutes with no goal but the copy.** Removes the pressure that causes the block. Boredom starts you within two minutes.
- **Working and creating are separate. Stop and leave.** The five-minute break is not rest — it's the half where connections get made.
- **Creativity is connectivity.** Join two things that already exist. You're not summoning; you're pairing.
- **Occupy your focused mind to free the rest.** Shaving, showering, holidays, a second project. Carry a pen everywhere.
- **Run three projects at once.** One feeds another. Ideas for A arrive while you're inside B.
- **Copy is assembled, not written.** If you feel short of words, you're actually short of claims. Go back and collect.
- **Words are windows. If you see the window, it's dirty.** The reader should see the product, never admire the sentence.
- **Find the feeling they haven't said out loud.** Not "people want less pain" but "people feel guilty about defying their doctor."
- **Line 1 makes the claim. Line 2 makes it doable.** Method, proof, and moving the risk off the reader — all in the second line.
- **Use surrogates.** Case histories are people who took the risk so your reader doesn't have to.
- **Every line's only job is the next line.** Frees the headline from selling. You're building a habit of reading.
- **Give a reason to open, and an exit to the order form.** Serve both readers: the one who wants everything, and the one leaving now.
- **Long enough to hold the claims, short enough not to bankrupt you.** The real answer to "how long should copy be."
- **Never sell. Ask them to try.** Shrink the decision. Higher returns are an acceptable price for more trials.
- **One person, sharing a problem with millions.** Intimate voice, mass market. Beginners usually lose one half or the other.
- **Change every "the" to "your."** A pass you can run mechanically. There is no the face — only your face.
- **Speak their vocabulary, even the technical parts.** Simple means their words, which are sometimes more technical than yours.
- **Listen — and fight your own success.** Rising takes you away from the people you sell to. Rebuild that contact deliberately.
- **Every three minutes, another punch.** Not paragraphs — a series of headlines. You owe them for their attention.
- **People live by hidden, sometimes shameful desires.** Name what they actually want, alongside what they'll admit to wanting.
- **Be melodramatic because attention is partial.** Make the painting big and high-contrast — then fill it with proof.
- **Claim against mechanism. Medium claim, strange method.** The engine. Strange enough that only the product can settle it.
- **"Can't be done" — they'll buy to prove you wrong.** Curiosity that survives disbelief. You no longer need to be trusted.
- **The more crowded the field, the harder you twist.** Claim exhaustion is structural. The mechanism carries what the claim can't.
- **You sell bundles of jobs, not products.** A tired advert doesn't need new words. It needs a different set of jobs.
- **Commitment predicts commitment.** How much effort someone spent becoming a customer predicts what they do next.
- **Always play position on the next sale.** Gifts, guarantees and generous trials are investments in the second order.

## What to question

Nothing here is a reason to dismiss the lecture. But he's teaching from one specific business — health books sold by post to an older audience in the early 1990s — and some of what he states as universal is unsupported, out of date, or contradicted elsewhere in his own talk. Knowing which is which is the difference between using this and copying it blindly.

**PROBABLY A MYTH — The Mozart billiard ball.** Presented as fact, with a physical explanation attached. It's a repeated story with no reliable source, and the claim underneath it — that Mozart never revised his music — is contradicted by his surviving manuscripts, which show plenty of revision. He also cites the film Amadeus, a dramatisation, as evidence. The technique is sound. The history isn't.

**ONE PERSON'S ACCOUNT — "27 of 30 — because I outwork them".** A writer describing his own record, with nothing to compare against. He was also being handed strong products by an exceptional publisher, which he half-acknowledges elsewhere. Adopt the preparation method; don't treat the causal claim as proven.

**HE CONTRADICTS HIMSELF — "Copy is never written" — then the four words arrived on their own.** Lesson 4 insists copy is only ever assembled. Lesson 5 describes his most famous line arriving whole, unassembled, from nowhere. He never reconciles the two. The sensible reading is that assembly is the part you can schedule, and it's what gets you deep enough in the material for the unplannable thing to show up — but he states the first as an absolute and then immediately breaks it.

**OUT OF DATE, BUT TRANSLATABLE — "The envelope has replaced the headline".** Literally true of physical post. The transferable principle is that your whole first surface is the pitch, and its job is to earn the open. Map that to whatever your first surface is — subject line and preview text, thumbnail and first three seconds. Don't map it to "cram fifty claims onto it."

**COMMONLY MISQUOTED — "No focus groups, no demographics".** He tested entire packages against each other on real lists and counted real orders — a demanding feedback loop. He is rejecting asking people in advance, not measurement. Anyone quoting this to justify shipping on instinct with no read on results has inverted his position.

**CHECK BEFORE REPEATING — The Joel Silver filmography.** He names Lethal Weapon (correct), plus Death Wish and Pretty Woman — neither of which Silver produced. The rhythm argument doesn't depend on the credits, but don't repeat the list as fact.

**THE QUESTION HE LEAVES OPEN — Guilt, "sneaky," and shameful desires.** The arthritis package works by locating readers' guilt about defying their doctors and settling it in favour of buying — in a health product. Schwartz's counterweight is genuine: be fair, be honest, fill it with proof, and he clearly respects his audience. But he never asks where the line falls between naming a hidden desire and exploiting one. In health and money copy especially, that judgement is left entirely to you — and it's the most important thing the lecture doesn't teach.
