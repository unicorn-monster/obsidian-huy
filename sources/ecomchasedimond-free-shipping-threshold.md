"I refuse to pay $6 for shipping," she says, and adds a $12 pair of socks to her cart.

Most of us have done some version of this.

Before she added the socks, her cart was $44. Free shipping started at $50, and the store had $12 socks hanging right next to checkout. All of that was planned.

She walks away feeling like she beat the system, and the store just added $12 to her order.

If you're setting this up, start with how much a typical customer spends per order. Use the median, since a few big orders can inflate the average. Set your free shipping minimum a little above that. Then, in the cart, show how far people are from free shipping and suggest a few small, useful items that get them there.

After that, check two things: that the extra profit from bigger orders covers the shipping you're now paying for, and that the free shipping minimum isn't so high that people give up and leave.

In a Bizrate Insights survey earlier this year, 56% of US shoppers said they expect free shipping once they spend $25 to $49, so most people already have a number in mind.
