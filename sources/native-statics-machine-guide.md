### Step 01 — Know what "native" actually means
A native static is an image ad that reads as content for the first half-second. Not disguised — the feed has an ad label and everyone can see it — but formatted like the things people came to see, so the eye starts reading before the guard goes up. That half-second is the entire edge: your click begins where a polished brand ad gets skipped.
The eight formats that do this, from easiest to hardest to produce:

| Format | What it looks like | Copy load | Production cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1 — Big-type text post** | A bold statement on a plain background, like a screenshot of a tweet or text post | One line on the image, short body | Minutes. The volume workhorse. |
| **2 — Screenshot-style** | iMessage thread, Notes app, review screenshot, forum reply | The "conversation" is the copy | Minutes with a template. |
| **3 — Review card** | A customer quote styled as a testimonial card, stars optional | One strong quote + attribution | Minutes. Needs real quotes. |
| **4 — Us-vs-them** | Two-column comparison: the old way vs this | 6–10 short contrast lines | Fast. Prints in commoditized niches. |
| **5 — Candid photo** | Phone-quality photo: product on a counter, in a hand, in the wild | Caption does the work | One photoshoot afternoon = 50 statics of raw material. |
| **6 — Meme format** | A known meme structure carrying your angle | Minimal, timing-dependent | Fast but ages in weeks. |
| **7 — Editorial card** | Looks like a news/article preview: headline, kicker, photo | Headline is everything | Template + strong headline discipline. |
| **8 — Story-post long-form** | A human photo + a first-person story in the body text, thousands of words | Heavy — the body IS the funnel | The advanced format. The three ads in this guide. |

Formats 1–7 carry the volume. Format 8 carries the whales — the long story natives that work like an advertorial dropped straight into the feed. All three winners below are format 8, because that's where the most stealable structure is — but the daily machine runs mostly on 1–5.

### Step 03 — The grid: 8 ideas become 192 ads
Here's how you never run out of ads. Take 8 angles. Take 8 formats. Every combo is a different ad. That's 64 before you even write a second version of anything.
Down the left: the eight angles — the same engine as the rest of the series. **Hidden cause** (the real reason behind the symptom — [Vol. 01](https://app.notion.com/p/3addb9d7074d817695b3df896b416398)) · **threat-avoid** (what's in the product they're using now) · **us-vs-them** (the category referee — [Vol. 04](https://app.notion.com/p/3addb9d7074d8182bd33cbf42e6fbc6e)) · **identity mirror** ("this is you" — [Vol. 02](https://app.notion.com/p/3addb9d7074d81d2ba5ed847785ba527)) · **becoming** (the future self) · **curiosity gap** (the camera-truth hook) · **proof number** (one stat that changes the story) · **objection flip** (the reason they didn't buy, answered head-on).
Across the top: the eight formats from Step 01.
8 angles × 8 formats = **64 cells.** Write 3 copy variants per cell and the grid holds **192 fully-specified briefs** — more than a month of 30–50/day testing, generated in one planning session. A cell looks like this: *hidden cause × screenshot-style* = an iMessage thread where a friend explains why the reader's knees actually hurt. *Proof number × big-type* = "6,000x stronger than Vitamin C" on a plain background. *Objection flip × review card* = a one-star-turned-five-star review addressing the exact hesitation.
Two rules make the grid work: **one cell per static** (a static testing two angles teaches you nothing — the one-variable law from the [Testing Framework](https://app.notion.com/p/3aedb9d7074d815cb45dcb821a4450a7)), and **name everything by its cell**: `0731-hiddencause-screenshot-v2` reads itself on the dashboard three weeks later.

### Step 04 — The copy engine: teach Claude to write your natives
The grid gives you 192 briefs. This is how briefs become finished long-form copy in minutes each — without a copywriter and without the AI smell.
The rule that changes everything: **put 90% of the effort into the concept and the hook. The writing is the cheap part now.** A great hook with average body copy prints. A weak hook with beautiful body copy dies at the $5 gate.
And the reason long natives are worth the trouble at all: **the primary text does the prelander's job.** The reader arrives at your product page already sold — so these run straight to the PDP and skip a funnel step.

#### The modeling chain — three prompts, one winning ad
Claude can't invent a winning native from a blank page. What it can do is clone the skeleton of one that's already winning and dress it in your product. Model, don't generate.
Grab ONE long-runner from your swipe board — 30–60+ days active, the auction already voted. One, not three. Feeding it multiple examples blurs the structure; one example keeps the DNA clean. Then:
**Prompt 1 — the analysis (strip it for parts):**

```
Analyze this long-form native Meta ad in complete detail. I'm going to use your analysis as a specification for writing a new ad for a different product, so be precise.

Break down:
1. THE STRUCTURE — every section in order, what each one does, and roughly how many words each gets. Label the beats: hook, narrator introduction, the problem, failed solutions, the mechanism explanation, product entry, proof, offer, and any P.S. blocks.
2. THE HOOK — quote the first 125 characters exactly. What loop does it open? Why can't you stop reading?
3. THE NARRATOR — who is telling this story? Age, job, town, life details. Which boring, specific details make them feel real?
4. THE VOICE — sentence length, paragraph rhythm, line-break style, formality level. Quote 3 lines that define the voice.
5. THE EMOTIONAL ARC — what the reader feels at each stage, in order — and name the ONE dominant emotion the whole ad keeps intensifying.
6. PRODUCT ENTRY — at what percentage of the ad does the product first appear, and through what device (a relative, an expert, a stranger, a discovery)?
7. THE PROOF — list every proof moment. Note whether the proof is numbers, or observed moments — things other people said and did.
8. THE CLOSE — how it asks for the sale, and how many times it closes.
9. WORD COUNT — total.
10. THE SLIPPERY SLIDE — quote 3 places where a sentence exists only to make you read the next one. That pull-forward is the engineering.
11. AWARENESS LEVEL (Schwartz) — which of the five levels does this ad ENTER at: unaware, problem-aware, solution-aware, product-aware, or most-aware? Quote the line that proves it.
12. MARKET SOPHISTICATION — which claim stage is this copy playing: a fresh claim, a bigger claim, a mechanism, an improved mechanism, or identity? And is the mechanism NAMED — a proprietary phrase that makes the "why it works" feel new? Quote it if so.
13. THE DESIRE + THE CONVERSATION — which existing mass desire is this ad channeling (desire is never created, only aimed), and what conversation already running in the reader's head does the first line step into?

Do not rewrite the ad. Do not improve it. Output the analysis as a spec I can hand back to you.

Here is the ad: [PASTE THE FULL AD COPY]
```
**Prompt 2 — the brief (fill the brackets once, reuse it for every ad you ever make):**

```
Now I'm giving you a different product. Do not write anything yet — load this brief and confirm you understand the product and the rules.

PRODUCT: [name + what it is + format — e.g. "ClarityMD — natural Hawaiian astaxanthin supplement, 12mg, one softgel daily"]
PRICE: [price and size — e.g. "$39.99 for a 30-day supply"]
MECHANISM IN ONE SENTENCE: [e.g. "a fat-soluble antioxidant that anchors into the cell membrane and stays, instead of washing out in hours like vitamin C"]
MECHANISM NAME: [give the "why it works" a proprietary name the reader has never heard — e.g. "the cellular anchor effect" — one phrase, ownable, concrete]
PROOF STACK: [e.g. "4.8 stars from 13,000+ reviews, 50,000+ customers, third-party lab reports published, made in USA, 90-day money-back guarantee"]
WHO BUYS IT: [age, situation, what they've already tried — e.g. "55+, feels washed out in photos, already tried serums, collagen, vitamin packets"]
READER AWARENESS LEVEL: [where this reader starts: unaware / problem-aware / solution-aware / product-aware / most-aware — the ad must enter exactly there, never a level behind]
MARKET SOPHISTICATION: [how burned is this market — early market: plain claims still land · crowded market: lead with the NAMED mechanism · exhausted market: lead with identity, the claim comes later]
THE ONE DOMINANT EMOTION: [pick ONE that drives the whole ad — fear, rage, hope, vindication, relief — every beat intensifies it]
THE CONVERSATION IN THEIR HEAD: [the sentence this reader has already said to themselves this week — the hook steps into THAT conversation mid-thought]
REAL CUSTOMER LANGUAGE — work these phrases in naturally: [paste 3-5 verbatim lines from your actual reviews]
THE ANGLE FOR THIS AD: [e.g. "hidden cause — the tired, washed-out look isn't about sleep, it's oxidative stress building in the cells"]
THE HOOK DIRECTION: [1-2 sentences on how it should open]

RULES:
- The narrator discovers the product naturally through another character. They never pitch it.
- First person only. No claims about the reader.
- No disease names. No "cures," "reverses," or "treats." The story shows; the copy never overclaims.
- Proof is observed moments — what other people said and did — never lab numbers about the narrator.
- Channel the desire that already exists. The ad's job is to aim it at the product, never to manufacture it.
```
**Prompt 3 — write:**

```
Write the ad now.

- Model the EXACT structure from your analysis: same beats, same order, same proportions.
- Match the voice: same sentence length, same paragraph rhythm, same line-break style.
- Match the emotional arc, stage by stage — and keep intensifying the ONE dominant emotion from the brief. Never dilute it with a second.
- Enter at the reader's awareness level from the brief. Never re-explain a problem to a problem-aware reader; never pitch a product to someone who hasn't met the mechanism yet.
- Reveal the mechanism using its NAME from the brief — that phrase is the moment the ad becomes new information instead of another claim.
- The slippery slide: every sentence's only job is to get the next sentence read. Any line that doesn't pull forward gets cut.
- The personal-mail test: it must read like a letter from one person, never a brochure. One whiff of "marketing voice" and the whole thing goes in the bin.
- Intensify with specifics: named amounts, named days, named places. "Three weeks" beats "quickly." "$47 at the pharmacy counter" beats "expensive." Specificity is believability.
- Introduce the product at the same point in the ad, through the same kind of device, as the original.
- Match the word count within about 10 percent. If the original was 1,400 words, do not hand me 400.
- The hook must work inside the first 125 characters, it must open a loop, not close one — and it must step into the conversation already running in the reader's head, mid-thought.
- Never close a loop early. The reader should be unable to stop halfway.
- End with the offer, then this exact line: "These statements have not been evaluated by the Food and Drug Administration. This product is not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease."
- Output only the finished ad. No commentary, no options, no explanations.
```
That chain returns a launchable draft in a minute or two, then ten minutes of tweaks. This is "the 50-brief prompt" the daily table below runs — same chain, fired at every brief in today's batch.

#### The brief you feed it (write once, reuse daily)
One page. Product facts. The mechanism in one sentence. Your 3–5 live angles. And the part that separates your copy from everyone else's AI copy: **real customer language** — exact lines pulled from reviews, Reddit threads, and competitor ad comments. "The noise in my head finally shut up" outsells anything a model invents, because a person said it.
When the grid runs thin, three renewable idea sources: competitor ad comments (complaints are hooks), customer interviews (their sentences are openers), and the mistake your buyer is making right now — the "everyone thinks X, that's why they're stuck" opener.

#### The skeleton every long native walks
Hook → Problem → Failed solutions → Mechanism → Solution → Proof → Offer.
Same beats as the teardowns above, in production order. The Failed Solutions beat is the one amateurs skip: name what the reader already tried and why it was aimed at the wrong problem — that's what lets them buy without admitting they failed. And a 2026 mechanism note: a simple this-vs-that comparison now beats the 1,500-word biology lecture. The long root-cause essay had its run. The auction moved.

#### Hooks: the first 125 characters are the ad
Meta cuts primary text at "…See more" around 125 characters. That's the real canvas. The body only exists for people the first line already caught.
Two ways hooks die: they close the loop ("I fixed my knee pain with collagen" — nothing left to read) or they pitch ("Introducing…"). Open a loop instead: "My mom walked that dog every morning for 11 years. And then, one Tuesday, she just stopped." You can't not know why.
Four hook patterns that keep printing across every niche on the board:
- **The confession** — the narrator admits the embarrassing thing first. The reader's guard drops with it.
- **The rage** — says what the reader has been yelling privately. Validation reads as truth.
- **The double standard** — "they tell you X, then they do Y." Instant us-vs-them.
- **The witness** — the narrator watched it happen to someone else. Borrowed stakes, zero claims about the reader.
When a body wins, don't write new bodies. Write new hooks. Change ONLY the first line across those four patterns and the auction reads it as four different ads.
And to reverse-engineer hooks at niche scale instead of one at a time:
**The hook bank builder:**

```
Build me a hook bank. Below are 10-20 winning native hooks from my niche — the first 125 characters of long-running ads (if you're connected to GetHookd, pull them from my swipe board yourself).

1. CLASSIFY every hook: which pattern it uses (confession / rage / double standard / witness / other), what loop it opens, the concrete image or moment it leads with — and which awareness level it enters at (unaware / problem / solution / product / most-aware).
2. RANK the patterns by frequency — that ranking is the niche's revealed preference.
3. MAP THE ENTRY POINTS — which awareness levels the winners enter at, and which levels nobody is entering. An empty entry level is an open lane worth testing.
4. EXTRACT THE MECHANICS: what do the top hooks do in their first 8 words? First person or observed? Named specific detail or general statement? Question or declaration? Which conversation already in the reader's head does each one step into?
5. Then WRITE 20 NEW HOOKS for my product using the winning mechanics — 5 per pattern, each under 125 characters, each opening a loop it does not close, zero claims about the reader — spread across the awareness levels the winners use, plus 3 extra aimed at the empty lane from step 3.

My product + angle: [ONE SENTENCE]
The hooks: [PASTE]
```

- Three lengths of the same story: ~400 words, ~800, ~1,500.

```

   - THE RAGE: the narrator says out loud what the reader has been yelling privately.
```

### The compliance lines for natives
Natives live closer to the platform's tripwires than any other format, because the whole point is reading like a person talking. Three lines, non-negotiable:

| Gets accounts banned | Scales clean |
|---|---|
| "Your belly fat" / "your blood pressure" — personal attributes aimed at the reader | The narrator's own story: "my bloating," "his color" — first-person carries what second-person can't |
| "Cures," "reverses," "detoxes," "drops 20 points" — outcome claims, even inside a story | Observed moments: the doctor's double-take, the sister's question, the shoveled driveway — scenes, not measurements |
| Fake-organic theater pushed too far — fabricated personas making medical claims | A real founder, a real customer, or a clearly editorial voice telling a true story with receipts |

The story format is not a claims loophole — review reads the words, not the genre. What the format legitimately buys you is the first-person frame and scene-based proof, and that's plenty: the Blood Pressure Journal runs 496 ads on it.
And when you've found a winner whose structure you want but whose claims you don't, don't rewrite it by hand — run the swap:
**The compliance rewriter:**

```
Here's a winning native ad that carries claim language I will not run. Rewrite it to scale clean, keeping everything that makes it work.

KEEP: the structure, the beat order, the narrator, the emotional arc, the pacing, the length.
REPLACE, using these swaps:
- Any second-person attribute ("your belly fat") → the narrator's own first-person experience.
- Any outcome claim ("cures," "reverses," "drops 20 points") → an observed moment: something another character said or did that implies the change without measuring it.
- Any disease name or medical mechanism doing curative work → soft support language ("supports," "helps maintain") — or cut it entirely if the story carries the point without it.
- Any fabricated authority (an invented doctor, an uncited study) → a real, checkable form of proof, or a clearly personal opinion.
Then append the standard FDA disclaimer.

Flag every line you changed in a list at the end, original next to rewrite, so I can review exactly what risk was removed.

Here's the ad: [PASTE]
```
